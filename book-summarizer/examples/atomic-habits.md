# Worked Example: Layered Summary

> Demonstrates the depth ladder on a widely known nonfiction title (*Atomic Habits* by James Clear). Shows how the same book is summarized differently by purpose.

## One-liner
Small, consistent improvements compound into remarkable results, and you change behavior by redesigning your systems and identity — not by relying on goals or willpower.

## Gist (one paragraph)
The book argues that habits are the compound interest of self-improvement: a 1% daily gain is trivial in a day and transformative over a year. Rather than setting goals (which winners and losers share), you should build *systems*. Habits form through a four-step loop — cue, craving, response, reward — and you change them by making good habits obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying (and bad habits the inverse). The deepest lever is identity: lasting habits come from becoming "the kind of person who" does them.

## Key takeaways (actionable)
1. **Focus on systems, not goals** — the goal is the result; the system is what produces it.
2. **Aim for 1% better** — tiny gains compound; you don't need dramatic change.
3. **Use the Four Laws** to build a habit: make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying.
4. **Habit-stack:** "After [current habit], I will [new habit]."
5. **Design your environment** so the right action is the easy one — beats willpower.
6. **Use the 2-minute rule** — shrink a habit until it's almost impossible to skip.
7. **Anchor to identity** — "I'm a runner" sustains longer than "I want to run."
8. **Never miss twice** — one slip is fine; two starts a new habit.

## Structure
A short conceptual foundation (compounding, systems, identity) followed by one part per law, each with tactics and stories, closing with advanced tactics (habit tracking, the Goldilocks rule of staying motivated at the edge of ability).

## Who it's for
Anyone wanting a practical, mechanism-level system for behavior change. Light on novel science; strong on actionable framework and memorable framing.

> Faithful paraphrase of the book's argument; consult the source for exact wording and the studies it cites.
