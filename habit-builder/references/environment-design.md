# Environment Design & Tracking

Willpower is a finite, unreliable resource. The people who seem to have the most self-control are usually the ones who structured their environment so they need it least.

## Reduce friction for good habits
- Put the cue in your path: book on the pillow, water bottle on the desk, guitar on a stand (not in a case).
- Prep in advance: gym clothes laid out, healthy meals pre-portioned, tabs open to the task.
- Reduce steps: the fewer actions between you and the habit, the more likely it happens.

## Add friction for bad habits
- Make the cue invisible: phone charging in another room, junk food out of the house, app deleted or behind a screen-time lock.
- Add steps: log out of accounts, unplug devices, leave the credit card at home for impulse spending.
- Use commitment devices: website blockers, automatic transfers to savings, a friend who holds the Netflix password.

## Track it
- **Visual streak** (calendar X's, an app, a paper chart). Seeing the chain motivates you not to break it.
- **Habit scorecard:** list your current daily habits and mark each +, –, or = relative to your goals to find what to change.
- Keep tracking itself *easy* — a tap or a checkmark, not a journal entry.

## The never-miss-twice rule
Missing once is an accident; missing twice starts a new habit. When you slip, the only goal is to do the *next* rep. Get back on the next day, even at the tiny version.

## If-then obstacle plans
Pre-decide your response to the predictable obstacle:
> "If it's raining and I can't run outside, then I'll do a 15-minute home workout."
> "If I'm too tired to read 10 pages, then I'll read 1."

This removes in-the-moment decision-making, which is when habits die.
