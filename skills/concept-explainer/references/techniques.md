# Explanation Techniques

## The Feynman technique
1. Explain it as if to a 12-year-old.
2. Find the spots where you stumble or reach for jargon — those are gaps in your own understanding.
3. Go back to the source, then re-simplify.
4. Use a plain-language analogy.

## Analogy quality rules
A good analogy:
- Maps to something the learner **already** understands.
- Matches the **structure** of the concept (relationships, not just surface).
- Has its **boundaries stated** ("this is where the analogy stops working").

Bad analogies share only superficial features and silently mislead. Always bound them.

## Progressive disclosure (layered depth)
- **Layer 0 — Gist:** one sentence a non-expert remembers.
- **Layer 1 — Working model:** a short paragraph enabling basic reasoning.
- **Layer 2 — Mechanics:** how it actually works, with an example.
- **Layer 3 — Nuance:** edge cases, trade-offs, when it fails.

Write all layers; let the reader stop where they like.

## Contrast and boundaries
Explain what something **is not** and how it differs from a neighbor concept (e.g. "authentication vs. authorization", "concurrency vs. parallelism"). Boundaries sharpen understanding.

## Concreteness ladder
Abstract → semi-concrete → concrete. Always land on a concrete instance: real numbers, a runnable snippet, a tangible scenario.

## Pre-empt the misconception
Most topics have one classic misunderstanding. Name it and correct it directly: "People often think X. Actually Y, because…"

## Check for understanding
- Ask a small application question.
- Offer a "you can now…" capability statement.
- Invite the next level of depth.
