# Competitive Analysis Frameworks

A toolkit of frameworks. Pick by the question you must answer — do not run all of them by reflex.

## Choosing a framework

| Question you're answering | Use this |
|---|---|
| How attractive/defensible is this market? | Porter's Five Forces |
| Where do we stand vs. named rivals? | Weighted comparison matrix + SWOT |
| Where is the unoccupied, valuable space? | Perceptual / positioning map (2x2) |
| What do customers actually hire products to do? | Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) |
| How do we differ on what buyers value? | Value-curve (Blue Ocean) |
| Why are we winning/losing deals? | Win/Loss analysis |
| What protects us long-term? | Moat taxonomy |

## SWOT (subject company vs. landscape)

Four quadrants — keep each bullet evidence-backed, not adjectival.
- **Strengths** (internal, helpful): assets, capabilities, share, brand that competitors lack.
- **Weaknesses** (internal, harmful): gaps a buyer would notice; table-stakes you miss.
- **Opportunities** (external, helpful): market shifts, underserved segments, competitor stumbles.
- **Threats** (external, harmful): new entrants, substitutes, pricing pressure, platform risk.

Rule: every Strength/Weakness should map to a dimension in your comparison matrix. Every Opportunity/Threat should link to a Five-Forces factor.

## Porter's Five Forces

Rate each force Low / Medium / High and justify in one sentence.
1. **Competitive rivalry** — number/strength of direct rivals, growth rate, differentiation.
2. **Threat of new entrants** — barriers: capital, network effects, switching costs, regulation.
3. **Threat of substitutes** — alternative ways to get the job done (incl. status quo).
4. **Buyer power** — concentration, price sensitivity, switching cost, ability to self-serve/build.
5. **Supplier power** — dependence on key platforms, APIs, data, talent, channels.

High on most forces = structurally hard market; pick your battles.

## Perceptual / Positioning Map (2x2)

Choose two axes the buyer genuinely trades off (NOT two things you're good at). Good axis pairs:
- Ease-of-use vs. Power/depth
- Price vs. Breadth of solution
- Specialist (vertical) vs. Generalist (horizontal)
- Self-serve vs. High-touch

Plot each competitor as a dot (size = market share). Read the map:
- **White space**: empty quadrant that buyers actually want → differentiation opportunity.
- **Red ocean**: crowded quadrant → expect price competition.
- **Stuck-in-the-middle**: dots clustered at center → no one owns a position; brand-able gap.

Render as ASCII for docs:
```
High power |  Jira        | Linear (us?)
           |              |
-----------+--------------+-----------
           |  Spreadsheet | Monday
Low power  |______________|___________
          Hard to use      Easy to use
```

## Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

For each segment, write the job statement: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Then map which competitor best serves each job. Wins are won at the job level; features are just the means.

## Value-Curve (Blue Ocean)

List 6-10 factors the industry competes on. Score each competitor 1-5 and draw the curves. Look for factors to **Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create** (ERRC) to break away from the pack rather than match it.

## Win/Loss Analysis

Structured interviews with won and lost deals. Capture: who else was evaluated, the deciding factor, price role, and the one thing that would have changed the outcome. The single highest-signal source of real competitive truth — weight it heavily.

## Moat Taxonomy

Classify each player's durable advantage:
- Network effects
- Switching costs / lock-in
- Economies of scale / cost advantage
- Brand / trust
- Proprietary tech or data
- Regulatory / distribution lock

No moat = differentiation is temporary; plan to keep moving.
